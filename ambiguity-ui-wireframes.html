Asking the doctor — four versions

The resolver now asks in exactly three situations, and each has a different shape of answer. They are not the same question, so they should not be the same control. Below: what actually triggers a question, four ways to render it, and which I would ship.

01

When it asks — and it is rarer than it was

3 triggers

After the resolver fixes, 37 of 37 benchmark cases resolve with zero wrong answers and zero questions. The common path asks nothing. What remains is genuinely uncertain, and the rate matters: severity-tiered alerting lifted compliance from 10% to 100% on the top tier, while flat alerting decayed to noise. Every question spent on an obvious answer devalues the one that isn't.

The trap to design against A doctor asked to choose between two identical-looking entries learns the questions are noise — and then clicks through the one that matters. That is why the resolver now prefers the single-molecule product and the oral solid automatically: a question with an obvious answer is worse than no question.

  • The question lives inside the card it belongs to. No modal, no context switch, and the dose and duration the doctor already dictated stay visible while they answer.
  • Full names on every option — generic, strength, form, route, and the brand underneath. Never truncated, because the best-evidenced e-prescribing error is picking the entry next to the one you meant.
Why this is the default It scales to any number of open questions without stacking modals, it never blocks the rest of the prescription, and the doctor can answer them in whatever order they like. With two medicines and one question, the other card is untouched and still editable.
Its weakness, stated plainly It is skippable. Nothing forces the doctor to look — they can scroll past and hit Review. The sign gate catches it (signing is refused while anything is unresolved), but the catch happens at the end, which is the worst moment to discover work remaining.

  • One question at a time, with the audio that produced it. The doctor answers a focused thing rather than scanning a form for amber borders.
  • “Your clinic uses this 43×” is real data — the usage count the catalogue already learns. It is a fact, not a recommendation, and it is the single most useful disambiguator a busy prescriber has.
Why it might beat A Real multi-step interaction cost measurably cuts override rates — the classic security-warning study saw dismissal fall from 90% on a one-click warning to 45% on a redesigned page with genuine friction. A queue is friction of exactly the right kind: it is proportional to the number of real questions, and it is zero when there are none.
Why it might not It is a mode. A doctor who wants to fix line 2 first has to get there, and a queue that appears for ONE question is heavier than that question deserves. Test it above a threshold — inline for one, queue for three or more.
04

Version C — the strength question, which is not a medicine question

ship as its own control
1. Clonazepamconfirm strength
You said 0.25 mg. This clinic's list has Clonazepam at 0.5 mg only.
why you are seeing thisWe will never quietly change a strength you stated. The medicine matched; the number did not.
  • “Keep what I said” is the default and listed first. The doctor is the prescriber; our catalogue being incomplete is our problem, not theirs.
  • This is a different question from "which medicine", so it gets a different control — two options, not a list, and no search box. Collapsing them into one generic "confirm" dialog is how a specific question becomes a generic click.
This question exists because of a real bug Before the fix, “Clonazepam 0.25” silently resolved to Clonazepam 0.5 mg — a twofold error on a controlled drug, presented as a confident match with a green tick. The UI showed no question at all, because the resolver did not think there was one.

Every decimal-dose medicine was affected: benzodiazepines, paediatric syrups, thyroid, digoxin. It is now impossible for the system to resolve to a strength other than the one spoken — it asks instead.
05

Version D — the one I would not build

anti-pattern
⚠ Please confirm medication
The AI is 72% confident about this medicine.
Accept AI suggestionDismiss
  • Looks more informative. Is measurably worse.
Three things wrong with it 1 · The percentage is fiction. Across 48 models, self-reported confidence exceeded accuracy in every single one. And in a real clinical task, high numeric confidence caused overreliance that reduced diagnostic accuracy. Gigerenzer's gynaecologists: 60% got a probability question wildly wrong in percentage form; 87% got it right as natural frequencies. Google PAIR and Microsoft's own HCI guidelines both warn against raw percentages.

2 · “Accept AI suggestion” is a rubber stamp with a default, which is the exact shape the habituation literature shows decaying with exposure.

3 · “Dismiss” must not exist here. Published CDS override rates run 49–96%. A question a doctor can wave away is not a control; it is a liability that looks like one.

Everything that asks, blocks. There is no dismissible tier anywhere in this design. A finding either prevents signing or is not shown — which is only affordable because the questions are now rare.
What to measure once doctors are using it Questions per prescription. If it climbs above roughly one in five, the resolver is asking too much and the answer is better data, not a better dialog.

Which option gets picked. If the first option wins 95%+ of the time, the question had an obvious answer and should become an automatic preference — exactly how "prefer the single molecule" and "prefer the oral solid" were derived.

Time-to-answer over tenure. If it falls while the mix of questions stays constant, that is habituation setting in, not fluency — and it is the signal to change the friction before someone clicks through a real one.
