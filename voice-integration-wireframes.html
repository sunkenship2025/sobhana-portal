Voice × editor — where dictation fits, given it is never 100%

You asked the right question. The interesting design problem is not how to put a microphone on the screen — it is what the interface does when the microphone is wrong, which it will be. This page maps the failure modes first, asks who actually catches each one, and only then proposes patterns. The wireframes are ordered by how much of the error they can absorb, not by how impressive they look.

01

Where voice actually fails — and who catches it

read this before the patterns

Every pattern further down is judged against this table. The column that matters is the last one: for several of these, nothing in the system catches it except the doctor's eyes, and the research is blunt that the eyes are unreliable — two deployed commercial scribes propagated 55% and 60% of planted upstream errors into finished notes.

The conclusion that should drive the design Of eight failure modes, the system reliably catches two. Four land entirely on the doctor, and one — omission — lands on nobody at all. So the interface cannot be “dictate, glance, sign”. Its job is to make the four doctor-caught failures visible in the two seconds a doctor will actually spend, and to shrink the window in which they can happen at all.

  • Absorbs: unknown-word errors and either/or. Both surface as badges before signing.
  • Misses: everything in the doctor-only rows above. A wrong strength on line 1 looks exactly like a right one.
The structural weakness A 40-second dictation is one long audio window, and the Whisper research found fabrications are disproportionately triggered by long pauses — which is exactly what a doctor does mid-prescription while thinking about the next drug. Longer audio is not linearly riskier; it is where the worst failures live.
And the review it invites is the weakest kind Three finished-looking cards appear at once. The research on fluency is direct: a polished, complete-looking draft inflates trust independent of whether it is correct. This is the layout most likely to be rubber-stamped on a busy morning.
Keep it, but not as the only mode. For a two-line prescription from a doctor who dictates cleanly, it is genuinely the fastest thing on this page. The error is treating it as the default for everything.
03

Pattern B — one medicine per press

the highest-value change

The mic lives on the “add a medicine” row, not above the whole prescription. One press, one utterance, one card. The doctor sees it land before speaking the next one.

Mid-prescription

1. Amlodipine 5 mgmatched confirmed by you
● listening for one medicine… Stop
hearing“Augmentin six twenty five three times a day after food five days”
+ Type a medicine ★ Favourites ↻ Repeat last Rx
  • Absorbs: a lot. A 6-second utterance has a fraction of the hallucination surface of a 40-second one, and there is no long thinking-pause inside it because the pause happens with the mic off.
  • Error scope is one line. When something is wrong, it is wrong in the card the doctor is looking at, not the second of three they already scrolled past.
Why this is the one I would ship first It changes the two variables that actually drive accuracy — utterance length and pause exposure — without asking the doctor to do anything differently. They still just talk. And it converts review from a single end-of-dictation event into a rhythm: speak, see, speak, see.

It also happens to be cheaper. Sarvam and Groq both bill by audio duration, and dead air between medicines is no longer recorded at all.
The cost, stated honestly Three medicines means three presses instead of one. For a doctor doing 40 consultations a day that is real friction, and some will prefer Pattern A. So both exist, and the doctor's last choice is remembered — personalisation is one of the four measured drivers of clinician satisfaction across 35,000 physicians.
04

Pattern C — voice searches, never writes

eliminates the worst failure

The drug name spoken by the doctor never becomes a value. It becomes a query. The card stays empty until the doctor taps one of the offered matches — so a wrong-drug error cannot survive to the sheet, by construction rather than by vigilance.

Voice → picker

you said“amlodipine five”
Tap the one you mean — nothing is selected.
Amlodipine 5 mg — tablet — oral Amlong 5
Amlodipine 10 mg — tablet — oral Amlong 10
Amiodarone 200 mg — tablet — oral ⚠ sounds alike · different drug entirely
Keep “amlodipine five” as typed
  • The look-alike is shown on purpose, and marked. Hiding it would not make the doctor safer — they would never learn the pair exists. Showing it separated and flagged is how a picker teaches instead of traps.
  • Dose, frequency, timing and duration still come straight from the same utterance — those are cheap to re-read and rarely catastrophic. Only IDENTITY gets the extra tap.
This is tiered friction, applied exactly where the evidence says to Severity-tiered alerting lifted compliance from 10% under flat alerting to 100% on the top tier — the single cleanest causal result in the research. Drug identity is the top tier. Everything else is not.

And it directly answers your point: voice is not accurate enough to choose a medicine. It is easily accurate enough to narrow a list to three.
The variant worth testing against it Auto-select when the match is unambiguous AND exact (a single catalogue hit, score 1.0), and only fall back to the picker otherwise. Faster, and arguably safe — an exact string match on a curated catalogue is not a guess.

I would benchmark both rather than argue. The measurable question is how often an exact-match auto-selection is later corrected by the doctor. If that number is near zero, take the speed.

Pattern D — transcript-first, structure derived

good for the hard cases

Show the transcript as the primary editable object. The doctor fixes the words, and the structured cards re-derive underneath. Fixing “as it real” to “Azithral” is a natural act; hunting for which field is wrong is not.

  • Absorbs: the self-correction failure, uniquely. “500, no make it 250” is visible as text, and the doctor deletes three words rather than hunting for a field that silently kept the wrong number.
  • The squiggle marks a span the resolver could not place — not a spelling error. It is the honest signal: “this looked like a medicine and I do not know it.”
Why this is a secondary mode, not the primary one Two representations of one prescription is a synchronisation problem, and the doctor now has to understand which one is authoritative. That is a real cognitive cost for what is, most days, an unnecessary safety net.

It earns its place as an escape hatch: a “show what I said” toggle that appears when something did not resolve, rather than the surface everyone starts on.
Hinglish is the reason this matters more here than elsewhere. Code-switched dictation is exactly where ASR degrades, and where a doctor is most likely to want to see the raw words rather than argue with a field. The research found in-domain adaptation worth 6 WER points against 1.9 for generic Hindi data — until we have that adaptation, the escape hatch is doing real work.
06

The one nothing catches — and the only honest answer to it

omission

If the doctor says four medicines and three are transcribed, no badge appears, no validator fires, and the sheet looks perfect. This is the largest error class in the literature and the one both machines and humans are near-blind to, for the blunt reason that an omission leaves no text to point at.

Count, stated out loud

3 medicines heard
You spoke for 22 seconds.
On the attestation:
I have reviewed all 3 medicines and I am prescribing them.

Cheapest, and already built. A number is harder to skim past than a list. It will not catch every omission, and it is not pretending to.

Play back what was heard

▶ 0:22
full transcript“patient ko azithral 500 BD five days dena hai, aur pantop forty subah khali pet, aur dolo 650 SOS…”

The only thing that genuinely detects an omission. Abridge ships exactly this. It also forces a decision about audio retention — you cannot replay what you deleted.

Ask, before signing

Anything else?
You usually prescribe 3–4 medicines. This one has 2.
That's allAdd one

I would not build this. It is a nudge based on a habit, not evidence, and it fires on every short prescription. That is how a control becomes noise — the 49–96% override path.

Say the limitation out loud rather than engineering around it There is no interface that makes omission safe. The honest posture is: keep the audio for a short retention window so playback exists, state the count, and tell the clinic plainly that dictation can drop a medicine and the doctor is the only check. A feature that quietly pretends otherwise is worse than one that says so.

Number 5 is the one that compounds When a doctor corrects “as it real” to Azithral, that correction is worth more than any model change: it is in-domain adaptation, one row at a time, for free. The research put in-domain adaptation at 6 WER points against 1.9 for 1,800 hours of generic Hindi. The cheapest accuracy mechanism we have is the one that improves without anyone retraining anything.
What to measure while testing, per pattern Not “accuracy”. These four:

1 · Correction rate per field. How often the doctor changes drug / strength / frequency after dictation. Per field, because a 5% drug-correction rate and a 5% duration-correction rate are not remotely the same finding.
2 · Time from stop to sign. Pattern B will lose on raw dictation time and should win here.
3 · Correction rate over tenure. If it falls while nothing else changes, that is habituation, not improvement — the PR-review study found approval rising 30% → 37% over seven months at flat difficulty.
4 · Abandonment. How often a doctor starts dictating and finishes by typing. The clearest signal that a pattern is not working, and the one they will never tell you about.
Test order. Groq first — it is ~8× cheaper and a free tier likely covers your volume — on the same ten recorded utterances through every pattern. Then Sarvam on the identical audio. The comparison is worthless unless the audio is the same, which is why the SpeechRecognizer adapter exists and why the prescription stores asrProvider and asrModel on every row.
